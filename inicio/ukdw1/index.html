The three-second rule

Give a room of kids an instruction and count how long you wait before you fill the silence yourself. Measured properly, most teachers get to about one second.

This has been studied since the 1970s. When that pause was stretched past roughly three seconds, the change wasn't subtle: more kids responded, the quiet ones started speaking, and far fewer instructions got ignored. Most teachers also believe they're already waiting four or five seconds. They're not.

What this looks like on a mat
Say it once, at normal volume. Then stand still and count one mississippi, two mississippi, three mississippi without moving, without repeating, and without adding "guys?"

Those seconds will feel unbearable. They are also the entire mechanism — you're giving fifteen children time to notice, process and comply, and most coaches interrupt that at second one and then conclude nobody listens.

Why repeating costs you

Every time you repeat an instruction that was ignored, you teach the room that your first instruction was optional. Do it enough and the class learns to wait for the third one — because the third one is the real one. That's how a coach ends up shouting by minute six.

Why explaining more makes it worse
Give a seven-year-old four instructions and they'll execute the last one you said, not the most important one. So the order that works is show, do, correct — and the third demonstration is watched by nobody.
Why "push the floor away" beats "use your legs"
Cue what the body does to the room instead of what the body does to itself, and movement and retention both improve. Two decades of motor-learning research, replicated with children, almost never used on a mat.
Why the far corner is always where trouble starts
Not because those kids are worse. Because it's the one place in the room where being off-task is free — and that's a positioning problem, which means it's yours to fix and it takes no discipline at all.

None of this is opinion. It's classroom and motor-learning research that's existed for decades and was never translated onto a jiu-jitsu mat. Sources are listed in the back of the guide.
